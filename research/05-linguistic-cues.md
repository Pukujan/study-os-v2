# Linguistic and Notational Cues: What Makes Content Hard or Easy

*Research digest for Study OS. Domain: measurable linguistic/notational drivers of comprehension difficulty, and which of them can be controlled in generation. Written for a builder; evidence graded as **[strong]**, **[promising]**, or **[thin/marketing]**.*

---

## 0. The one framing to carry throughout

Difficulty is **not a property of text**. It is a property of the *interaction* between text features and a specific reader's vocabulary, prior knowledge, and notation familiarity. The best-documented demonstration is the **expertise reversal effect**: the same scaffold that helps a novice (high cohesion, worked examples, simplified wording) can *hurt* an expert, and vice versa (Kalyuga/Renkl line; e.g. the statistics self-explanation study, [10.1007/s10734-011-9476-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10734-011-9476-1)). Every finding below should be read as "population-average effect," not "for this learner." This is the central gap the whole field has.

The unifying mechanism is **cognitive load theory** (Sweller): what matters is *element interactivity* — how many things a learner must hold and relate at once. Jargon, unfamiliar notation, and long-distance dependencies all raise element interactivity. Simplification lowers it for novices and can strip the very structure experts rely on.

## 1. Readability formulas: what they capture and what they miss

**What they are.** All the classic formulas are surface proxies:
- **Flesch Reading Ease** (1948) and **Flesch-Kincaid** (1975): average sentence length + average syllables/word.
- **Dale-Chall** (1948): % of words *not* on a 3,000-word "easy" list + sentence length. The 3,000-word list is the closest thing here to a vocabulary model — and it's an 80-year-old list of 4th-grade familiarity.
- **Gunning Fog, SMOG, ARI, Coleman-Liau**: variants of the same two knobs.

Correlations with comprehension tests are decent *in aggregate* (Dale-Chall 0.93, Flesch-Kincaid 0.91 against McCall-Crabbs cloze norms, per the [Wikipedia Readability entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Readability)) but the standard errors are ~1.8–2.3 grade levels, and the formulas are validated only on averaged passage sets, not individual passages.

**What they miss.** Word length is an unreliable proxy for semantic difficulty; sentence length is an unreliable proxy for syntactic complexity; and the formulas ignore content, purpose, discourse structure, and reader knowledge entirely ([AHRQ, "Use Caution With Readability Formulas"](https://www.ahrq.gov/talkingquality/resources/writing/tip6.html)). Crucially, **they do not measure comprehension** — they measure text features that correlate with it on average. A short, punchy sentence full of undefined jargon scores as "easy."

**Coh-Metrix** (Graesser, McNamara, McCarthy, Cai, 2004, [10.3758/bf03195564](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03195564)) is the serious alternative: a multi-dimensional battery computing word frequency, syntactic complexity, **referential cohesion** (argument/lexical overlap), **connectives**, situation-model/coherence indices, narrativity, and word concreteness. The **Coh-Metrix L2 Reading Index** (lexical coreferentiality + syntactic sentence similarity + word frequency) predicted reading difficulty for L2 learners *better than traditional formulas* in both [Crossley, Greenfield & McNamara 2008](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1545-7249.2008.tb00142.x) and [Crossley et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.64152/10125/66657), which found it classified beginner/intermediate/advanced text significantly better than Flesch-type formulas. **T.E.R.A.** (Text Easability Assessor) packages five Coh-Metrix dimensions as a usable UI.

**The limit even here:** even Coh-Metrix explains only a *modest* share of variance in actual comprehension, because the missing term is the reader. Modern readability assessment has moved to supervised models and LLMs (CLEAR corpus of ~5,000 teacher-rated excerpts, [10.3758/s13428-022-01802-x](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-022-01802-x); CEFR-annotated corpora), but the labels themselves are subjective, rater-dependent, and often derived from the same surface features being criticized. **There is no validated reader-relative ground-truth difficulty measure.** This is the biggest open question in the domain.

## 2. Lexical factors — the strongest single lever

**Word frequency [strong].** High-frequency words are processed faster and more accurately; this is one of the most replicated effects in psycholinguistics (Brysbaert et al. 2018, [10.1177/0963721417727521](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721417727521)). Use **SUBTLEX**-style corpus frequencies (subtitle corpora beat older norms) on the **Zipf scale** (1 ≈ 1-per-100M, 4+ = high frequency) ([SUBTLEX-UK, 10.1080/17470218.2013.850521](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470218.2013.850521)). Caveats: frequency estimates disagree; **semantic diversity** and **word prevalence** (how many people know it) predict processing beyond raw frequency. Concreteness ratings ([Brysbaert et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-013-0403-5)) are free and downloadable — see §4.

**Lexical coverage thresholds [promising, not law].** Hu & Nation (2000) proposed that L2 readers need to know **98%** of running words for adequate comprehension, 95% for minimal; that implies ~8,000–9,000 word families for unassisted reading. This "98%" figure is cited everywhere and is *load-bearing* for design — but Kremmel et al. 2023 failed to fully replicate it in a non-WEIRD sample ([10.1111/lang.12622](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12622)). Treat coverage as a useful gradient, not a threshold; **it is text-relative-to-reader**, which is exactly the reader-relativity problem again. What *is* robust: text coverage (fraction of words a given learner knows) is the single best predictor of whether that learner can read a text.

**Technical vocabulary load [strong].** Nation (2003) found technical words made up **1 in 3 running words in an anatomy text and 1 in 5 in applied linguistics** ([10.64152/10125/66770](https://doi.org/10.64152/10125/66770)). Many technical words come from the first 2,000 words + the **Academic Word List** (Coxhead) — these are **subtechnical / semi-technical** words: ordinary-looking words ("field," "pressure," "current," "solution") carrying domain-specific meaning. Subtechnical vocabulary is widely regarded as *the* hardest tier — abstract, image-less, and collocation-driven — and the main barrier in ESP/technical reading. For a Two Sum / Big O context, the load is not just "algorithm" and "complexity" but words like "sorted," "optimal," "hash," "iterate." The **Technical-Subtechnical-Nontechnical (TSN) model** ([10.1002/trtr.2375](https://doi.org/10.1002/trtr.2375)) is a practical planning tool for triaging which tier a given word sits in.

**Jargon is not the same as rare.** A term can be frequent in English and still be a hard technical term ("list," "node," "key"). Frequency alone will misjudge domain texts; you need a domain-aware term model.

## 3. Syntax and discourse

**Sentence length ≠ syntactic complexity [strong].** This is a common conflation and it matters. The driver of syntactic difficulty is *embedding depth, dependency distance, and noun-phrase density*, not raw length. Two consequences: (1) splitting long sentences can *raise* load by fragmenting the logical relation; (2) a 2011 study of third-graders' science texts found **lexical complexity affected comprehension but syntactic complexity did not** — for young learners, vocabulary dominated. (I could not verify this study's DOI; it is worth reading in the original before leaning on it.) For adults in technical content the balance shifts, but the lesson holds: don't assume shorter = easier.

**Connectives and cohesion [promising, reader-dependent].** Cohesion is "the degree to which ideas are explicitly related." Adding causal connectives ("because," "therefore") tends to improve comprehension and recall, but evidence shows the benefit appears mainly **when the text is already coherent**; in incoherent text a connective can't repair the gap, and adding it can *increase* reading time (French-language study, [10.4000/cpl.1146](https://doi.org/10.4000/cpl.1146)). The most robust cohesion finding is the **expertise-reversal interaction**: high referential cohesion helps low-knowledge readers and can *hurt* high-knowledge readers, who are forced to process relations they'd have inferred anyway (McNamara's high/low-cohesion work; reinforced by text-picture cohesion studies, [10.3389/fpsyg.2020.592509](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.592509)). Referential-cohesion skill predicts reading-comprehension achievement in school-age readers.

**Given-new / information structure [thin].** Clark & Haviland's (1977) given-new contract — put known information first, new information in the stressed/final position — is a well-taught *principle* with mixed empirical support; comprehension depends on the reader's ability to *assign* given/new roles, and many learners perform at chance on this even for passives and clefts ([study](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0305000900006292)). Cite it as a heuristic, not a law.

**Examples and analogies [thin, and double-edged].** Analogy is a genuine explanatory tool and can improve conceptual knowledge and overcome misconceptions via "bridging" analogies ([physics study](https://doi.org/10.7275/13472430)). But: learners under load fixate on **surface similarity** rather than structural mapping (Richland et al.), and analogies can *degrade* metacomprehension accuracy — students who rely on analogies judge their understanding poorly ([Wiley et al. 2018, "When analogies harm"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959475217303067)). Do not write §3 as "analogies help." They help when the structural mapping is explicit and the learner has the prior knowledge to see it.

**Seductive details [strong].** Interesting-but-irrelevant adjuncts (fun facts, tangents) *reduce* recall of main ideas and problem-solving transfer; the damage is worse when placed early ([Harp & Mayer 1998](https://doi.org/10.1037//0022-0663.90.3.414)). A subway-learner app has a strong incentive to add "engaging" color; this is a measured cost.

**Worked examples [strong].** Novices learn more from studying worked examples than from solving equivalent problems, and the benefit **reverses for high-prior-knowledge learners** — the canonical expertise-reversal case ([10.1007/s10734-011-9476-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10734-011-9476-1)). The implication: the right representation for step N is not the right representation for step N+1, for the same learner.

## 4. Notation and symbols as linguistic objects

**Mathematical notation [promising].** Notation is not neutral packaging. Duval's **registers of semiotic representation** framework ([book, 10.1007/978-3-319-56910-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-56910-9)) argues the difficulty is *converting between registers* (algebraic ↔ graphic ↔ verbal ↔ tabular), not the content. **Symbol sense** (Arcavi, 1994) names the informal mastery of when/how to use symbols. Kuchemann (1981) showed students treat algebraic letters as *specific unknowns* rather than variables — a representational, not conceptual, failure. The equals sign is read operationally ("compute the answer") rather than relationally. All of this is measurable via standard misconception inventories.

**Code identifier naming — the best-evidenced, most controllable knob here [strong for speed, untested for learning].** This is directly relevant to the project's founding anecdote. The controlled-experiment literature:
- **Hofmeister, Siegmund & Holt 2017** (SANER): 72 professional C# developers, within-subjects defect-finding. **Words → ~19% faster comprehension than letters or abbreviations; no significant difference between letters and abbreviations** ([10.1109/saner.2017.7884623](https://doi.org/10.1109/saner.2017.7884623)).
- **Schankin et al. 2018** (ICPC): 88 Java developers. **Descriptive compound names → ~14% faster** semantic-defect finding, with eye-tracking evidence (more time on the lines *before* the defect, fewer regressions). **The effect disappeared for syntax-error tasks** (no deep understanding needed) **and did not appear for less-experienced developers** ([10.1145/3196321.3196332](https://doi.org/10.1145/3196321.3196332)). *This novice null is a direct tension with a learner-facing product — state it.*
- **Beniamini et al. 2017** (ICPC): controlled experiments found **no significant comprehension difference for single-letter names** in small scopes, and showed single letters do carry conventional meaning (`i`, `x`, `y`) ([10.1109/icpc.2017.18](https://doi.org/10.1109/icpc.2017.18)).
- **Sharif & Maletic 2010**: eye-tracking, underscore identifiers recognized **faster** than camelCase (no accuracy difference) ([10.1109/icpc.2010.41](https://doi.org/10.1109/icpc.2010.41)).
- **Butler et al. 2010**: full-word identifiers → best comprehension.
- **Newman et al. 2025** ([arXiv 2505.18444](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.18444v3)): closed-class words in identifiers (prepositions, determiners, conjunctions, numerals) encode *relations and behavior*; open-class words name *entities*. This is the most linguistically sophisticated work in the area.
- Anchor review: Herka 2025, *Identifier Names, Comprehension and Code Metrics* ([10.5709/acp-0479-3](https://doi.org/10.5709/acp-0479-3)).

**Why the `seen` → `box` anecdote is NOT explained by "descriptive names are better."** `seen` is arguably *more* descriptive of what the variable holds (things already seen). The renaming literature would predict little from `seen`→`box`. The better-supported explanation is a different variable: **concreteness/imageability**. `box` is a concrete, imageable noun; `seen` is an abstract participle. The **concreteness effect** (concrete words processed faster, recalled better — Paivio dual coding; Brysbaert et al. 2014 concreteness norms, [10.3758/s13428-013-0403-5](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-013-0403-5)) is one of the most replicated findings in psycholinguistics. A secondary, **speculative** mechanism: `box` names an *entity the learner can act on* (open it, look inside), while `seen` names a *property of elements* — a shift from property-naming to object-naming that changes the affordances the name suggests. This connects to Newman et al.'s finding that identifiers encode relations and behavior, not just referents. The mental-model/metaphor story is coherent but has **no direct code-comprehension evidence**. It is n=1. **What would test it:** a controlled experiment crossing concreteness × descriptiveness × length in identifier renames, holding algorithm, structure, and length constant. Nobody has run it; it is cheap and Study OS could run it on its own learner data.

**Reading code is not reading prose [strong].** Novices read code *less* linearly than natural language; experts read it less linearly still ([Busjahn et al. 2015, 10.1109/icpc.2015.36](https://doi.org/10.1109/icpc.2015.36)). Notational layouts (crowding, spacing) change gaze behavior, with particular impact on readers with dyslexia ([McChesney & Bond 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10664-018-9649-y)).

## 5. Controllable difficulty in generation

**The corpora.** **Simple English Wikipedia** pairs (Coster & Kauchak 2011, ACL) and **Newsela** (Xu, Callison-Burch & Dolan 2015) — professionally written news articles at 5 reading levels, licensed, not freely redistributable. **Newsela-auto / Wiki-auto** (Jiang et al. 2020, [10.18653/v1/2020.acl-main.709](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2020.acl-main.709)) rebuilt larger, better-aligned sets via a neural CRF aligner. **OneStopEnglish** (189 texts × 3 levels, CC BY-SA 4.0, [10.18653/v1/w18-0535](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/w18-0535)) is the freely usable one.

**The corpora are themselves error-ridden [strong, and builder-critical].** Devaraj et al. 2022 (*Evaluating Factuality in Text Simplification*, [10.18653/v1/2022.acl-long.506](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2022.acl-long.506); [PMC9671157](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9671157/)) built a taxonomy of insertion/deletion/substitution errors and found: in Newsela test pairs, **only 15.8% had no deletion error and 42.9% had severe (level-2) deletions**. Models *add* substitution errors beyond the data. **SARI correlates only weakly with factuality**, and an automatic error classifier reached F1 as low as **9.5 for severe substitutions**. In a medical simplification case study, **3 of 10 paragraphs contained a severe error**. This poisons both training and evaluation: a system trained to imitate Newsela learns to delete content, and the standard metric won't catch it.

**LLM readability control [promising].**
- **ReadCtrl** (2024, [arXiv 2406.09205](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.09205v1)): instruction-tune a 7B model on simplification + paraphrase + entailment with a grade-level token; achieves the lowest readability gaps (e.g. 1.66 grade levels on unseen MRPC) and beats GPT-3.5/GPT-4/Claude-3 on factuality and SARI; human win rate 52.1% vs GPT-4's 35.7%. **Limits: English-only; standard formulas mis-fit medical text; human raters were university-educated, not the actual grade-2/5/8/11 audience.**
- **Stanford SCALE** (Day et al. 2025, [report](https://scale.stanford.edu/ai/repository/evaluating-genai-simplifying-texts-education-improving-accuracy-and-consistency)): 60 passages, 12th → 8th/6th/4th grade. **No model or prompting technique reliably hit the target grade while preserving keywords and length**; accuracy degraded worst at 4th grade.
- **Generating Educational Materials with Different Levels of Readability using LLMs** (2024, [10.1145/3690712.3690718](https://doi.org/10.1145/3690712.3690718)): few-shot prompting helps readability *and* meaning preservation, but manual inspection found **misinformation introduction and inconsistent edit distribution**.
- **Controllable simplification** (Kumar et al. 2021, [10.18653/v1/2021.naacl-main.277](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2021.naacl-main.277)): hybrid rules + neural paraphrasing lets you control *how much* of each operation is applied.
- **Alva-Manchego et al. 2020 survey** ([10.1162/coli_a_00370](https://doi.org/10.1162/coli_a_00370)): the field's benchmark; documents the **simplicity-vs-faithfulness trade-off** that is still unsolved.
- **Cripwell et al. 2024** ([arXiv 2404.03278](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03278v1)): evaluating simplicity and meaning preservation *separately* confirms the trade-off — "none of these four models ranks first on both dimensions," and all systems **undersimplify** relative to human references.

**Verdict:** you can steer readability with LLMs, but (a) you cannot yet hit a target level reliably *and* preserve meaning, (b) the ground-truth corpora are factually noisy, and (c) readability targets are the wrong objective — see §0.

## 6. Per-language considerations

- **Formulas don't transfer.** Flesch-family constants are English-specific (syllable counting is meaningless for Chinese; word segmentation differs for Japanese/Thai). Multilingual work uses CEFR-aligned or model-based assessment instead.
- **ReadMe++** (2024, [10.18653/v1/2024.emnlp-main.682](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2024.emnlp-main.682)): 9,757 sentences in Arabic, English, French, Hindi, Russian; shows **cross-lingual transfer is real and improves with multilingual training**, and that unsupervised methods have clear shortcomings. Follow-up work (2026, [arXiv 2609.10792](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2609.10792)) shows transformers recover length/syntax/lexical-diversity signals unevenly across languages and layers.
- **CEFR-based corpora** are the practical standard: CEFR-SP (17k professionally annotated English sentences, macro-F1 84.5% for level assessment, [10.18653/v1/2022.emnlp-main.416](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2022.emnlp-main.416)); CEFR-Cymraeg (Welsh); Estonian learner-text level prediction; a German corpus with typography/structure metadata.
- **Multilingual simplification is nascent but moving**: MultiMSD (2025) covers nine languages; multilingual medical simplification ([arXiv 2305.12532](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.12532v4)) is a good model of the approach.
- **L2 learner concern:** difficulty for L2 readers is dominated by **lexical coverage relative to their vocabulary size** — again the reader-relative term. A text's absolute readability is nearly meaningless without a vocabulary estimate for the learner.

## So what for Study OS

1. **Do not build on readability scores as ground truth.** Flesch-Kincaid is a surface proxy; Coh-Metrix/T.E.R.A. is better but still reader-blind. Any "difficulty" number Study OS computes is a population-average feature estimate, not this learner's difficulty. Say this to yourself every time a difficulty target appears in the design.
2. **The strongest controllable levers, ranked by evidence:** (i) **lexical** — frequency (Zipf), concreteness/imageability, and technical/subtechnical term density are measurable with free resources (SUBTLEX, Brysbaert concreteness/concreteness norms, AWL, domain term lists); (ii) **identifier and symbol naming** — the best-evidenced notational knob, with real 14–19% effects on comprehension *speed*, but untested for durable learning and *null for novices* in the one study that checked; (iii) **worked-example structure** — strong, and it interacts with expertise; (iv) **cohesion/connectives** — real but reader-dependent and can backfire.
3. **The `seen`→`box` anecdote is a research lead, not a settled finding.** The measured candidate is **concreteness** (concrete nouns are processed faster and remembered better); the mental-model/metaphor story is plausible but unevidenced. Study OS's own learner data can run the falsifying experiment: rename variables along concreteness × descriptiveness × length, hold everything else constant, measure both immediate comprehension and delayed retention. If concreteness is the driver, that is a directly generatable, per-item knob.
4. **Design for expertise reversal explicitly.** The scaffold that helps a novice (worked examples, high cohesion, simplified vocabulary, `box`) can hurt a learner who has moved past it. The controller needs a *representation ladder* per concept, not one "easy" setting. The brief's old architecture (deterministic controller owning representation authority) is well-matched to this — the finding supports it.
5. **If you use simplification corpora, expect factual damage.** Newsela pairs are ~43% severely deletion-errored; SARI won't catch it; auto-detectors are weak. Verify simplified output against the source with an entailment/QA check rather than trusting a readability score.
6. **Beware seductive details.** A distraction-friendly mobile context creates pressure to add engaging color; measured cost is lower main-idea recall. Keep the content tight.
7. **What would falsify the whole linguistic-cues thesis for Study OS:** if, in your own A/B data, representation changes (renaming, notation swaps, cohesion edits) produce no reliable learning gains beyond noise, or gains that don't transfer to delayed retention, then representational friction is not the bottleneck and the project's thesis is wrong. That is the experiment worth running early.
