# 03 — How systems know what is easy vs. hard

**Domain:** difficulty modeling, learner-response data, and where the data comes from.
**Date:** 2026-10-12. Sources verified during this session; every load-bearing claim links to its primary source.

## The thesis: difficulty is a triangle, not a property

"Easy vs. hard" is not a property of content. It is a joint property of **content × learner × time**. The modeling traditions in this field each operationalize one side of that triangle:

- **IRT** models *content* (item difficulty b, discrimination a, guessing c) against a population of learners.
- **Elo-style systems** model *content × learner* online, one update per interaction.
- **Knowledge tracing** (BKT/DKT/SAKT) models the *learner's trajectory* — what they know now, given their history.
- **Memory models** (SM-2, FSRS, half-life regression) model *time* — how recall decays and recovers.

A working product almost always needs all four, at different weights, and the honest finding of this research is that the *simple* end of each family is the right **starting point** in production — Elo-class rating, extended BKT, FSRS — while the learned sequence models (LSTM/Transformer KT) pay off only at scales and data densities most products never reach (see §3 for the counter-evidence: Duolingo retired its Elo-like model for an LSTM; Riiid runs deep KT in production).

## 1. Item Response Theory (IRT)

The canonical models. **1PL/Rasch**: P(correct) = σ(θ − b) — one difficulty parameter `b` per item, one ability θ per person ([Columbia PHM overview](https://www.publichealth.columbia.edu/research/population-health-methods/item-response-theory)). **2PL** adds discrimination `a` — the slope of the item characteristic curve, i.e., how sharply the item separates strong from weak learners ([Hambleton-style explainer](https://assess.com/irt-item-difficulty-parameter/), [Brown 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5978598/)). **3PL** adds a guessing floor. Estimation is joint maximum likelihood (JMLE) or Bayesian (MCMC) over response matrices.

**The cold-start problem is structural.** Every item is a free parameter; a new item has no data, so it cannot be scored, exposed adaptively, or compared to others. This is not an implementation detail — it is why large assessment programs maintain expensive pilot-testing pipelines (which also leak items: a security problem, per [McCarthy et al. 2021](https://research.duolingo.com/papers/mccarthy.emnlp21.pdf)).

**The modern fix that actually shipped** is Duolingo's BERT-LLTM ([McCarthy et al., EMNLP 2021](https://research.duolingo.com/papers/mccarthy.emnlp21.pdf)). It descends from the **linear logistic test model** (LLTM, Fischer 1973): instead of one free b per item, decompose b = **v**·φ(item) — difficulty as a weighted sum of features (here, 768-dim BERT passage embeddings). Difficulty parameters are *shared across items* via features, so unseen items get a prior immediately and live responses refine it. Trained multi-task with CEFR ordinal regression on 100,495 Duolingo English Test sessions over 4,151 C-test items: matches or beats 2PL-IRT on 4/5 test-taker metrics despite far fewer parameters; 2PL-IRT cannot score unseen items at all, while BERT-LLTM generalizes to them by design (jump-started item-mean r = 0.80 with the joint objective; 0.74 using test-taker responses alone). Crucially — the sentence a small builder will misread — **the paper's small numbers are about *model calibration*, not per-item recalibration**: "as few as 500 exam sessions" sufficed for near-optimal model performance in the data ablation, and jump-started new items needed ~3,000 pilot administrations for good performance with a >4,000-item bank (vs. ~200× more administrations for 2PL-IRT). The text-feature prior itself was trained on the 100k-session corpus.

**DINA / G-DINA** is the other item-explanatory tradition: given a Q-matrix mapping items to skills, estimate per-item slip/guess parameters and produce a skill-level mastery profile ([Stan case study](https://mc-stan.org/learn-stan/case-studies/dina_independent.html), [CDM R package](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/CDM/html/din.html)). Interpretable, but the Q-matrix is the real cost — expert annotation or learned Q-matrix inference is its own research problem ([Chung 2014](https://academiccommons.columbia.edu/doi/10.7916/D8J96DR6/download)). **Deep IRT** (neural nets estimating person/item parameters, e.g., [Tsutsumi & Nakamura 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/10/9/1020)) is an academic-benchmark phenomenon; I could not verify the widely-cited NEURAL/PSYCH benchmark sizes this session.

## 2. Elo-style systems: the production default

[Pelánek, *Applications of the Elo rating system in adaptive educational systems* (Computers & Education 2016)](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf) is the definitive treatment (heavily cited — 142 on Crossref, higher on Scholar). Frame: a student answering an item is a match between two "players." Same functional form as Rasch; the difference is the update rule — one SGD step per answer, rather than batch MLE. Key empirical findings:

- **Selection bias in logged data is the trap.** Under adaptive item selection, proportion-correct estimates of item difficulty are badly biased *and do not improve with more students* (Pelánek's simulation, §3). Elo with an uncertainty schedule matches JMLE closely in the same setting. **The moment a product serves items matched to ability, raw per-item accuracy rates become corrupted difficulty estimates** — which is exactly why online-updating models (Elo) are preferred over batch MLE there.
- **Convergence is fast where it matters**: once item difficulties are stable, ~10 answers per student yield skill estimates at r ≈ 0.8 with ground truth (simulation, Rasch-generated data — label accordingly).
- **Recommended starting point**: uncertainty U(n) = a/(1+bn) with a=1, b=0.05; "more complex variants can improve performance, but the improvement is often small and may not be worth the implementation complexity."
- Honest limits: Elo gives no discrimination or guessing parameters (it is 1PL only); JMLE itself is a modest bar (biased for short tests); real learners are non-stationary, which favors Elo over batch IRT but means item difficulties drift and the update needs decay.

**Duolingo is the proof at scale.** [IEEE Spectrum's Birdbrain piece](https://spectrum.ieee.org/duolingo) (Bicknell, Brust, Settles): Birdbrain v1 was "a flavor of logistic regression inspired by item response theory... a generalization of the Elo rating system" — one SGD step per completed exercise, difficulty estimated by summing component-feature difficulties (exercise type, vocabulary words). Ran at 1B exercises/day. V2 replaced the scalar ability with an LSTM producing a **40-dimensional ability vector**, because scalar ability couldn't capture patterns like "strong in past tense, weak in future tense." Pre-Birdbrain, difficulty was guessed from surface features (word counts).

**TrueSkill** ([Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TrueSkill); Herbrich, Minka & Graepel 2006) adds a Gaussian uncertainty (μ, σ²) per player via factor-graph message passing — the principled version of Elo's uncertainty heuristic — but it is patented/trademarked (patent expiring April 2029) and documented use is matchmaking, not item difficulty. **Glicko-2** (Glickman; glicko.net) is the same idea — rating deviation + volatility, no patent — and is common in chess-style and adaptive-testing contexts. Either gets the Elo-plus-uncertainty shape; a plain Bayesian update over (mean, variance) is enough for a small product.

**Item selection is a separate, load-bearing subsystem.** An adaptive engine needs (a) a difficulty prior for each item, (b) an ability estimate per learner, and (c) a *selection rule* balancing informativeness against exposure — because always serving the maximally-informative item both corrupts the difficulty estimates above and depletes the item bank. Assessment engines use exposure-control constants; Duolingo used prediction-based rating to avoid piloting ([McCarthy et al. 2021](https://research.duolingo.com/papers/mccarthy.emnlp21.pdf)).

## 3. Knowledge tracing: BKT, DKT, SAKT

- **BKT** (Corbett & Anderson, *Knowledge Tracing: Modeling the Acquisition of Procedural Knowledge*, UMUAI 4:253–278, 1994): a 2-state HMM per skill with four parameters (prior, learning rate, guess, slip). No forgetting by default. Runs per-skill, needs only a response stream, and its parameters have psychological meaning.
- **DKT** (Piech et al. 2015, [paper](https://geometry.stanford.edu/lgl_2024/papers/pbhgsgs-dkt-15.pdf)): LSTM over the interaction sequence; reported a 25% AUC gain over BKT on ASSISTments. **Then [Khajah, Lindsey & Mozer 2016, "How Deep is Knowledge Tracing?"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.02416) showed most of that gap evaporates**: classic BKT re-evaluated properly scores 0.73 AUC (not the reported 0.67 — an evaluation-procedure bias accounts for ~32% of the claimed gap), and BKT extended with forgetting + latent student ability + skill discovery matches or beats DKT on most benchmarks (BKT+FSA 0.90 vs DKT 0.86 on ASSISTments with exercise-indexed labels). Their conclusion: "knowledge tracing may be a domain that does not require 'depth.'" This is a rebuttal paper and the field moved on — but the load-bearing point for a builder stands: **the deep-vs-shallow accuracy gap is small and contested, not the reason to choose a KT model.**
- **SAKT** ([Pandey & Karypis 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06837)): self-attention over past interactions, +4.43% AUC on average over DKT/DKT+/DKVMN across ASSISTments 2009/2015/2017, Statics, and synthetic data — motivated by *sparsity*. Newer transformer KT models (AKT, SAINT) push this further, but gains are small relative to the gap between all of them and BKT.
- **DAS3H** ([Choffin et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.06873)) is the useful hybrid: additive factors + per-skill forgetting curves, modeling both skill tagging and memory decay. It bridges the KT and spaced-repetition worlds.

**Can a small-scale product use these?** BKT: yes, trivially (an HMM per skill; pyBKT exists). DKT/SAKT: honestly no — they need thousands of interaction sequences per skill to beat the shallow models and buy opacity. The realistic deep-learning role at small scale is none; use Elo + BKT. (Counterweight: Duolingo *did* replace its Elo-like model with an LSTM once it had a billion daily exercises, and Riiid runs deep KT in production — depth pays off at volume, not at launch.)

**What researchers learned about difficulty structure from these:** (a) difficulty estimates replicate across model families — Elo, JMLE, and feature-based models agree on Rasch-generated data and (per [multi-concept Elo at LAK 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07908)) Elo's exam-outcome accuracy is comparable to logistic regression on real data; (b) item difficulty correlates strongly with lexico-grammatical complexity features (McCarthy et al. found BERT-difficulty estimates "correlate strongly with lexico-grammatical features that correlate with reading complexity"); (c) **difficulty distributions are badly skewed** — most items cluster at low difficulty with few hard ones (a problem the [BEA-2024 USMLE study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12230038/) had to fix with aggressive augmentation), which means any self-generated content will have the same skew; (d) Eedi's misconception-tagged distractors showed that *wrong answers carry diagnosis signal* that binary correctness throws away.

## 5. Cold start: how new content gets its first difficulty estimate

The published pipelines converge on one architecture: **text features → prior → recalibration from live data.** The general statistical recipe underneath is **hierarchical / partial pooling** — item difficulties are drawn from a shared distribution conditioned on features, so a new item is pulled toward the mean of its feature-similar peers rather than starting at zero. This is the principled version of "jump-starting," and it is available off the shelf (Bayesian IRT with item covariates, e.g. in PyMC/Stan) without needing a transformer at all.

1. **Expert tagging** — the traditional route (CEFR labels; McCarthy et al. used 3,826 passages labeled by two experts, weighted κ = 0.599 — note the *mediocre* inter-annotator agreement on difficulty itself).
2. **Supervised text features → external scale** — Settles et al. 2020 (cited in [McCarthy et al. 2021](https://research.duolingo.com/papers/mccarthy.emnlp21.pdf)) predicted CEFR levels from linguistic features; useful, but it's supervision on a *proxy* scale, not on learner responses.
3. **Direct prediction from responses with feature sharing** — McCarthy et al.'s BERT-LLTM (§1), the strongest published version: features give the prior, responses give the truth, one model does both on the same logit scale.
4. **LLM-predicted difficulty**: the 2024–2025 evidence is unflattering for zero-shot use. A systematic review of 37 studies ([Peters et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.23486)) reports best-case Pearson r up to 0.87 / RMSE 0.165 — but those maxima come from *fine-tuned transformer* models on large assessment data, not from prompting. On the BEA-2024 USMLE shared task (667 items), fine-tuned BERT (RMSE 0.2926) beat GPT-4 with in-context learning (0.3556), and GPT-4 rationales/CoT added nothing ([Li et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12230038/)). I found no published end-to-end *zero-shot LLM difficulty → live recalibration* pipeline. **Honest status: "LLM predicts difficulty, then data calibrates" is industry folklore without strong public replication; the published evidence supports fine-tuned encoders, not prompted LLMs.** One extra warning for AI-generated content: using the same model to write items *and* rate their difficulty produces correlated errors — the estimates inherit the generator's blind spots.

## 6. Forgetting curves and memory models

- **Ebbinghaus (1885)**: the forgetting curve replicates ([Murre & Dros 2015 replication](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4492928/)) — steep early decay flattening over time, fit by power/logarithmic/summed-exponential forms — but the original was n=1 (himself), and his specific equation transferred poorly to other subjects. The *shape* is solid; any *specific* curve from a textbook is not. Recent work argues empirical curves for real study material deviate further from the ideal form ([Kornell et al. 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/15/7/924)).
- **SM-2** (SuperMemo, [Wozniak's original description](https://www.supermemo.com/en/blog/application-of-a-computer-to-improve-the-results-obtained-in-working-with-the-supermemo-method)): per-card ease factor EF, updated from 0–5 quality ratings; interval grows ×EF. Hand-tuned, per-card, no global model. Its known failure mode ("ease hell" — ease factors spiraling down) comes from having no mean-reversion mechanism.
- **FSRS** ([open-spaced-repetition](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/free-spaced-repetition-scheduler), MIT-licensed, [technical explanation](https://expertium.github.io/Algorithm.html)): the DSR model — **D**ifficulty (1–10, updated with mean reversion), **S**tability (interval at which recall probability = 90%), **R**etrievability (a power-function forgetting curve). 21 parameters (w0–w20), fit by gradient descent on log-loss treating every review as binary success/lapse. **Data requirements:** historically Anki required 1,000 reviews to optimize, then 400 (Anki 24.04); since Anki 24.06.3 there is no hard minimum ([FAQ](https://docs.ankiweb.net/faqs/frequently-asked-questions-about-fsrs), [tutorial](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/blob/main/docs/tutorial.md)) — but Anki's own docs still flag "less than a few hundred reviews" as a condition where FSRS underperforms. New users start from **default parameters = the median of 20k real parameter sets** (the FSRS-Anki-20k dataset) — i.e., *population priors until personal data accumulates*, which is exactly the cold-start pattern as §5. Difficulty is implicit: harder cards get smaller stability increases, so intervals grow more slowly.
- **Half-life regression** (Duolingo; [Settles & Meeder, ACL 2016](https://research.duolingo.com/papers/settles.acl16.pdf)): p = 2^(−Δ/h), with h = 2^(w·x) fit to data. Trained on **12.9M student-word practice traces**; cut MAE 45%+ vs Leitner/Pimsleur baselines. The engagement story is subtler than the abstract suggests: Experiment I (HLR vs Leitner, ~1M students, 6 weeks) *decreased* practice sessions −7.3% (strength meters stopped demanding pointless review) with only marginal gains elsewhere; the +12% daily-activity result (p < 0.001) came from Experiment II (3.3M students) and was for **HLR-lex vs HLR — i.e., *removing* the lexeme-difficulty features**, which had overfit and frustrated users. Treat both as single-product A/B results, not replicated effects. The load-bearing finding for a small builder: **lexeme/difficulty features "failed to improve predictions much" (the paper's own words), and interaction counts alone carried nearly all the signal** — and removing them *improved* engagement. Cheap, and directly encouraging for per-user-history-first design.

**Response time** deserves a note: it is a second, cheap difficulty/ability signal with its own modeling tradition (log-normal RT models; van der Linden's joint RT-accuracy IRT), and several of the datasets above (ASSISTments 2012/2017, EdNet) ship duration fields. Most small products collect it and never use it.

## So what for Study OS

**The practical ladder, given ~zero to small data:**

1. **Scheduling first.** For anything retrieval-shaped, use SM-2 or FSRS. FSRS is MIT-licensed, has ports in 10+ languages, and works from population defaults on day one while encoding difficulty implicitly. If a Study OS lane is vocabulary/facts (much of HESI A2), this is the highest-value, lowest-risk component.
2. **Add Elo per item and per learner for adaptive selection.** ~20 lines of code; it is what Duolingo shipped at 1B exercises/day (v1). Use Pelánek's uncertainty schedule (U(n)=a/(1+bn), a=1, b=0.05). But Elo needs a reasonable *initialization* — pair it with difficulty priors from elsewhere or new items are exposed blindly.
3. **Cold-start priors from fine-tuned text features, not from prompted LLMs.** The published evidence favors fine-tuned encoders over GPT-4 prompting (BEA-2024: BERT 0.29 vs GPT-4+ICL 0.36 RMSE). Architect so live responses override the prior within tens–hundreds of interactions, and never let the model that generates an item be the only thing rating its difficulty.
4. **BKT yes, DKT/SAKT no** — until there are tens of thousands of interaction sequences per skill, deep KT buys opacity without accuracy (Khajah 2016). BKT per skill, or Elo-per-concept, captures most of the value.
5. **Guard against the adaptive-selection trap.** Once items are served matched to ability, proportion-correct difficulty estimates are biased and *stay* biased (Pelánek §3). A naive "success rate per item" dashboard in an adaptive product is lying; Elo-style updates or explicit correction are mandatory.
6. **Map lane → model**: HESI A2-style content ≈ assessment → IRT/Elo thinking; DSA (procedural) ≈ KT/Elo-per-concept; vocabulary/facts ≈ FSRS/HLR. Don't force one model across all three.
7. **Data strategy**: no open dataset hits the sweet spot of *content + learner responses + text* for a new product. EdNet (CC BY-NC) and Eedi are the best open response-streams for prototyping; FSRS-Anki-20k is the only open spaced-repetition corpus and is where FSRS's own defaults come from — a template for bootstrapping priors from a community corpus.

**What would falsify the ladder:** a production system where DKT/SAKT materially beats tuned BKT/Elo at small scale (overturns point 4); a published zero-shot LLM difficulty pipeline beating fine-tuned encoders on held-out items (softens point 3); or evidence that proportion-correct stays unbiased under adaptive selection in real data (removes point 5's urgency).

**Biggest open question in this domain:** how good a cold-start difficulty prior can be made *cheaply* for AI-generated content. Every published pipeline leaned on large live-response volumes (100k+ sessions for Duolingo's calibration); nobody has published the "LLM-generates + small-scale-recalibrates" loop for a product with hundreds of users, not millions.
